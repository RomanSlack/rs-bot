## What changed today

One thing on the robot: the wheel servo got the cradle it has been missing, so
it is no longer held on by nothing. Everything else that changed today was a
CHECK, a CONSTANT or a DOCUMENT that had stopped describing the robot.

That is the theme, and it is worth stating before the detail. Yesterday was
about the CAD being wrong against a real servo on a real desk. Today the parts
were right almost every time and the things measuring them were wrong:

- a check that measured a screw's distance to its servo from the case centre
- a check that took a hole's position from the centroid of a trimmed face
- a test whose bound was set to the number the bug was producing
- the sim, quantised to 0.01 mm while the CAD is not
- four constants restating a bought part's box, all stale, one by 12.5 mm
- an FEA loading a joint that had been deleted the day before
- an order sheet, a mass budget and a blocking list, all quoting dead numbers

Seven of those eight could not fail, because nothing compared them to anything.
The four new checks added today all failed on their first run, which is the
only reason to believe any of them.

> **The parts are now better verified than the things that verify them.** That
> is the actual state of this project, and today was mostly about closing that
> gap rather than moving any geometry.

## 2. It was measuring a screw's distance to its servo from the case

`is_horn_screw()` decides whether a screw belongs to a servo's **horn**, which
matters because horn joints are sub-assembled: you bolt the part to the horn on
the bench, then push the horn onto the spline. Those screws are never fitted on
the robot, so asking whether a driver reaches them there is the wrong question.

It decided it by perpendicular distance from the servo's axis. The axis it
used ran through `_servo_frames`' origin, which is the geom centre, which is
the **centre of the case**. The shaft is `SHAFT_X` = 12.5 mm from there.

So it was measuring from a line 12.5 mm away from the shaft, against a
threshold of 12.0. On the roll bracket that split one four-screw pattern down
the middle:

| screw | from the case centre | from the shaft |
|---|---|---|
| upper pair | 9.03 | 7.00 |
| lower pair | **18.14** | 7.00 |

Two passed, two failed, and the two that failed were the two furthest from the
case centre rather than the two furthest from anything real. The pattern is a
perfect square of `HORN_DX` x `HORN_DY` about the roll axis, so from the shaft
all four sit at exactly 7.00 mm, which is what a horn pattern is.

**What it would have cost.** Nothing at the bench, and that is the point worth
recording: it cost *attention*. `docs/road-to-order.md` lists tool access as
item 3, and the four screws have been sitting in every status report since as
a known outstanding defect on a part that bolts up fine. A false red is not
free. It spends the same worry a true one does, and it trains you to skim the
check that finds it.

The dangerous direction is the other one, and it was live. Anything within
12 mm of a line through the case centre was **excused** from the reachability
check entirely. A genuine case screw could land inside that radius and never be
asked whether a driver reaches it, and the check would print green.

## 3. And the hole positions were the centroids of trimmed faces

Found on the way to the above, because the first measurements did not add up.
`holes()` took each hole's position from `f.center()`, the centroid of the
cylindrical face. A hole's barrel gets trimmed by every fillet, channel and
boolean that touches it, and the centroid of what is left is not on the axis.

The roll bracket's four horn holes came back **1.60 mm** low, a quarter of the
pattern's own radius, on a part whose geometry is exactly right. The axis
cannot be trimmed, so it now reads `f.axis_of_rotation` and projects the
centroid onto it: exact across the hole, still sensible along it.

This one is worse than it looks, because it also moved where the **driver was
stood**. Every reachability result in this repo was computed with the tool
planted up to 2 mm off the screw it was supposed to be turning.

## 4. One place that knows where a servo's output is

Both bugs are the same bug: `toolaccess` worked out where a shaft was instead
of asking. `cad/drives.py` already had the answer, correct and tested, since it
exists specifically to check that a shaft is on its joint.

So `drives.shaft_lines(mode)` is now exported and `toolaccess` imports it.
`_shaft_line` offers two candidates, because which way `SHAFT_X` points along
the case is recorded nowhere in the sim; `shaft_lines` resolves the sign
against the joint the servo drives, which is strictly better than the
nearest-of-two the old code took, and it is now resolved in one place instead
of two.

`drives` reports identical numbers to before the refactor, all ten servos, both
poses, which is what makes it a refactor.

`HORN_NEAR` is now `HORN_OD / 2` = 9.97, off the measured horn, rather than the
round 12.0 it was. The old threshold was wider than the horn it was testing
for.

## 5. The test around it had been pinned to the wrong answer

`test_every_screw_can_be_reached_when_it_is_fitted` asserted
`len(blocked) <= 4`, with a comment explaining that four per side on the roll
bracket sit under the ankle yoke and are "tight rather than impossible".

They are not tight. They are on the bench. The bound had been set to the number
the check happened to be reporting, which is a bound that cannot fail, and it
came with a paragraph of reasoning that made the wrong number sound considered.

It asserts zero now. Both tools, driver and hex key, reach every screw.

> **A threshold set to the number you already have is not a threshold.** It is
> a screenshot of a bug. Yesterday's rule was that a check that cannot fail is
> worse than no check; this is the same rule pointed at the number instead of
> the logic.

`cad.toolaccess`'s `main()` also returned 0 no matter what it found, so the one
check in this repo that was actually red reported success to anything that
asked a program rather than a person. It exits non-zero now, like `cad.drives`.

    8 screws reported unreachable       must be > 0    ok

## 7. The whole sim was rounded to 0.01 mm, and one part was built to 0.001

Taken next: `test_every_servo_seats_flat_on_what_it_bolts_to` had been failing,
1.9 mm3 of roll bracket inside the wheel servo's case. Confirmed pre-existing
against a clean tree, identical to the cubic millimetre.

Yesterday's guess was that this was the fifth servo mount, the one left without
a cradle because the roll arm's frame and `envelope.WHEEL_SERVO`'s frame
disagree. It is not. The frames are fine. What the intersection actually is:

```
   x  -35.11 .. 0.00     y  73.50 .. 84.30     z  52.365000 .. 52.370000
   area 379.19 mm2       thickness 0.005 mm    volume 1.896 mm3
```

A five-micron sliver spread over the whole seating face. Two faces meant to be
coincident, 0.005 mm apart.

**Every geom in the sim is written to `:.5f` in METRES.** That is a 0.01 mm
grid, and `cad/` is on no grid at all. The servo's real width is 24.73, so its
half-width 0.012365 m formats as `0.01237` and the sim's case comes out 24.74
wide. The roll arm seats at exactly `WIDTH / 2` = 12.365, because it is derived
from the one source of truth like it should be. So the more correct the CAD
got, the more certainly it landed inside the sim's rounded box.

| | true | sim at `.5f` | sim at `.6f` |
|---|---|---|---|
| LENGTH | 45.23 | **45.22** | 45.23 |
| WIDTH | 24.73 | **24.74** | 24.73 |
| HEIGHT | 36.50 | 36.50 | 36.50 |

**The width is the one that showed. The length is the one that matters.** It
rounds DOWN, so every clearance in this robot involving the side of a servo
case has been checked against a case 0.01 mm thinner than the real part. That
is the dangerous sign, the one the goal note calls out by name: a sim box
smaller than the thing it stands for is a gap that exists in the model and not
on the bench. Nothing was looking for it, and nothing would have.

0.01 mm is not nothing here. This project has already been bitten twice by
standoffs of 0.5 mm and 0.035 mm, and it caught the 0.035 one by watching a
seated-face count. Six decimal places costs nothing and puts both dimensions
exact.

Re-run after the change: drives, twin, fasteners, wiring, assemble_check,
fitcheck and toolaccess all clean, 16 seated faces still seated, and the
seating test passes.

> **The bug was in the model's precision, and it surfaced as an accusation
> against a part.** Worth remembering the shape of it: a check said "this
> bracket is eating a bought servo", and it was telling the truth about what it
> could see. Everything it could see was 0.01 mm wrong.

## 8. Three CAD copies of a bought part, all stale, none checkable

With the suite green for the first time, the next item was the one 08-01 left
open: pin `envelope.WHEEL_SERVO` against the sim before anyone designs the
fifth servo cradle against it. Measured, in the ankle frame, against the sim's
own geom:

| constant | drift | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| `envelope.WHEEL_SERVO` | **12.500 mm in x** | still centres the case on the wheel axis |
| `envelope.ROLL_SERVO` | **3.500 mm in z** | centres the case on a literal 16.0 |
| `ankle.ROLL_SV` | **3.100 mm in x** | the STEP's 39.6 shaft span, superseded by 36.5 |

The first one is `SHAFT_X`, for the **third** time in two days. The sim made
this exact mistake with this exact servo and had it corrected on 2026-08-01.
`cad/toolaccess.py` made it this morning. The envelope was never told either
time.

> **When a number turns out to be wrong in one file, the question is not
> whether it is fixed. It is who else has a copy.** Three for three now.

Why none of them could be caught: `assemble_check` and `fitcheck` run on the
sim's boxes and the real solids, and never look at these constants at all.
They are design-time reservations, so being wrong makes the NEXT part wrong
rather than this one. `envelope.py` is the file that decides which bands the
ankle-pitch bearing may sit in, and it was offering 12.5 mm of space forward
that the wheel servo does not use while hiding 12.5 mm aft that it does.

`ROLL_SV`'s 3.1 mm is on the face the yoke never builds from, which is why it
survived. The two that actually bit are the small ones: 0.035 in y and 0.085 in
z are on the faces the cradle IS built from, so its walls stood 0.035 mm off
the case they are meant to grip. That is the same standoff fault this design
has now been bitten by at 0.5 mm, at 0.035 mm, and this morning at 0.005 mm.

**`cad.twin` now has a second check**, `bought()`: for every CAD constant that
restates a bought part's box, does it still match the sim's geom, to 0.01 mm.
All three failed on the first run, which is the only reason to believe it.
Control-tested by nudging one box 0.02 mm and confirming exactly one entry
went red.

The fix is derivation rather than correction. Every servo dimension in
`envelope.py` now comes from `servo_dims`, every servo position is written
about `SHAFT_X` rather than a case centre, and `ankle.ROLL_SV` is deleted
outright: it was `envelope.ROLL_SERVO` stated a second time, so it now imports
it. One box, one place, and a check that fails if that stops being true.

`envelope.ANKLE_SERVO_SHIN` is **not covered and says so in the output**. It is
given in the shin frame, and `cad/shin.py`'s origin is not MuJoCo's shin body
origin, so asserting it would mean asserting a frame conversion nobody has
verified. Named rather than left silent, because an unchecked constant that
nobody lists reads exactly like a checked one.

## 9. The fifth cradle, and the sim's weight

With the box pinned and checked, the wheel servo finally got the mount it has
been missing since yesterday. Until now it was attached to the roll bracket by
**nothing at all**: its two case bolts were deleted on 08-01 for running
parallel to the face they were meant to clamp, and no cradle replaced them.

**It is a C, not a rim, and that is the design.** The other four cradles grew
from a plate on the case's end face. This mount is different: the arm already
lies across the servo's +z face, and the couple is about the shaft, which is y
here, so it is reacted by forces in x-z. A wall under the case completes an
opposed pair with the arm doing the other half. No new plate.

The x-pair was the alternative and it is worse. The aft wall is free, but the
forward one would sit at x = +10.1 where the arm ends at x = 0, so it would
mean growing the arm 13 mm outboard. That is a proportions change to buy a
joint a web already provides.

**And it stood in a cable, exactly like yesterday's four.** 156 mm3 of new wall
through the roll-to-wheel run, 2 of 8 blocked where the robot had been at 0 of
8 in both poses. The cause is ordering: the channel was cut before the cradle
existed, so it was subtracted from nothing. Same shape as the fillet that ate a
servo seat yesterday. The channel is now cut **last**, after every feature that
could stand in it, and the order stops mattering.

Verified: 0 interfering pairs both poses, nothing collides through the flip on
real solids, 0 of 8 cable runs blocked in both poses, still fits the bed, no
thin features, overhang 17% to 18%. FEA on the changed part puts it at
**70% of PA6-CF allowable against the 94% `road-to-order.md` records**, because
the cradle adds material in the load path. It costs 1.3 g per side.

**Then the mass check that did not exist.** `SEG_MASS` in `src/rsbot/model.py`
is a hand-copied snapshot of what `cad.masses` derives, and its own comment
says to re-run that module. Nothing re-ran it:

```
   thigh     sim   90.0 g   cad   96.3 g    -6.3
   shin      sim  124.9 g   cad  128.9 g    -4.0
   torso     sim 1077.2 g   cad 1085.1 g    -7.9
   total     sim 1874.8 g   cad 1907.1 g   -32.4
```

29 of those 32 grams are **yesterday's four cradles**. The status report that
day recorded their cost exactly - "mass went 1875 g to 1904 g" - into a
document rather than into the model. The sim is still the pre-cradle robot, and
it is what the balancer is tuned against. The missing mass is not spread
evenly either: it is on the legs, furthest from the pitch axis, where it does
the most to inertia.

`cad.twin` has a third check for it now, per body rather than in total, because
two errors in opposite directions cancel in a total and a total is what people
read. It is RED, deliberately, and it is the next item.

## 10. And the box the check had excused itself from

`bought()` shipped with one constant listed as not covered:
`envelope.ANKLE_SERVO_SHIN`, on the grounds that it is written in the shin
frame and `cad/shin.py`'s origin was not pinned against MuJoCo's shin body. It
was named in the output rather than left silent, and the note said it was the
obvious place for a fourth copy to be hiding.

It was. **6.8 mm in z, the largest drift of the four.**

```
sim  x -12.365..12.365   y 34.000..70.500   z -55.815..-10.585
cad  x -12.365..12.365   y 34.000..70.500   z -62.600..-17.400
```

Look at what x and y do there. They agree to a thousandth of a millimetre,
which settles the frame question outright: **two axes cannot match that
precisely through an unverified frame conversion.** The shin frame was pinned
the whole time. The excuse for not checking was not merely wrong, it was
disproved by the first line of the answer, and it had been protecting a 45.2 mm
span - the product-listing length the drawing superseded weeks ago.

> **The reason a check gives for not checking something deserves the same
> suspicion as the check itself.** This one sounded rigorous, named a real
> precondition, and was refuted by thirty seconds of measurement.

`NOT_COVERED` is empty now, and the check reads either frame: a joint anchor
for constants in world terms, a body offset for constants in a link's frame.
Control-tested on the new path by nudging the box 0.03 mm and confirming only
that entry went red.

The bands `cad/envelope.py` computes come out unchanged: the ankle-pitch
bearing centre can sit at y = +58..+80, and the yoke's boss spans 56..66 with
its centre at 61, comfortably inside. The correction moves what the file
reasons WITH, not the conclusion it had reached.

## 11. The FEA was loading a joint that was deleted a day earlier

Having said `SHAFT_X` was wrong in four files, the obvious next question was
where else a number like that is typed rather than derived. The grep turned up
one live case and it is not a servo box.

`cad/stress.py` loaded the roll bracket through **two hardcoded patches of arm
"where the wheel servo must bolt through"**. Those bolts were deleted on
2026-08-01, for running parallel to the face they were meant to clamp. The FEA
had been putting the entire wheel load into a joint that was never built, and
it is the load case behind the 94% figure `road-to-order.md` quotes as one of
the two thin margins in the project.

It now derives the interface from `cad/ankle.py`'s own cradle constants, so it
follows the part instead of drifting away from it.

**The first attempt did not converge and the reason is worth keeping.**
Selecting all three cradle faces - seat, bottom wall and web - gave a residual
34x the applied load. Distributing one wrench over two OPPOSED faces lets the
solver put equal and opposite tractions on them, which the constrained hub
barely sees: that is a mechanism, not a load case. It is also physically wrong.
The cradle's walls are a RETAINER - they stop the case lifting and take the
couple in bearing once it does - while the wheel's steady load presses the case
INTO the seat. A face that only bears when the joint is already moving is not
where the working load goes.

Seat only, and it converges. **The peak is 40.1 MPa, identical to the old
number.** So the correction did not move the answer; it moved the reason,
which had been resting on features that do not exist. 70% of PA6-CF allowable,
against the 94% recorded before the cradle added material.

## 17. "Is that a real hole?" had no answer but eyesight

A chunk of geometry looked like an artifact, and there was no way to settle it
except by looking harder. That is the wrong tool. `cad/printability.py` checks
overhangs, thin walls and bed size, and never asked whether the mesh it was
measuring is closed.

It does now. A closed surface uses every edge exactly twice, so a watertight
mesh has `edges == 1.5 * triangles` exactly, and all seven parts do:

```
thigh  shin  ankle_yoke  roll_bracket  chassis  wheel_body  wheel_tyre
                       all closed
```

This matters more here than on a desk printer. **An open edge is the first
thing a print service's own checker rejects**, and a rejection costs a
fortnight and a re-upload rather than an afternoon.

Two things worth recording about getting there:

**The first version of the check reported holes in all nine meshes.** It read
`[1:]` off the 12-float STL record instead of `[3:]`, keeping two components of
the facet normal and shifting every vertex by one float. Every edge came out
unshared, which looks exactly like catastrophic mesh damage and is in fact a
one-character slice bug. A check that fails everything is as useless as one
that passes everything, and it is more alarming, so it gets believed faster.

**Rounding is what makes it work at all.** STL stores float32 per triangle with
no shared-vertex table, so the same physical corner arrives with slightly
different bits from each face meeting there. Compared raw, nothing matches
anything.

Control-tested by deleting one facet from the thigh: 0 open edges becomes 3.

And the answer to the original question: **the chunks are cable channels.**
`cad.wiring.channel()` subtracts the run in wheel mode AND foot mode, so each
one is the union of two tubes along diverging paths and comes out forked rather
than as a tidy groove. 890 mm3 out of the shin, 388 out of the roll bracket,
362 out of the thigh, 396 out of the yoke. Deliberate, and it does not look it.

## 18. The sweep on real solids had never actually run

`docs/road-to-order.md` item 5 wanted the flip sweep re-run with tolerance
applied instead of at nominal. Writing it turned up something worse than the
thing it was written for.

`cad/assemble_check.py` had `stack()`, `sweep()` and now `sweep_stack()`, and
`uv run python -m cad.assemble_check` **called none of them**. The entry point
did `main()` on the two rest poses and stopped. So the flip sweep on real
solids - written yesterday, described in yesterday's report as "the first time
this robot has been swept on its actual geometry" - only ever ran when somebody
imported it by hand and remembered to.

> A check nobody runs is not a weaker check, it is an absent one, and it is
> worse than absent, because the documents list it among the checks that pass.
> This is the same family as a check that cannot fail, and this repo has now
> found both in two days.

Moving the entry point also surfaced why it was where it was: `sweep` is
defined further down the file, so calling it from a `__main__` block in the
middle raised `NameError`. The block is at the end now.

All four run from one command, and it is clean.

**The stack through the flip:** nothing closes at any point. The tightest pair
is the roll bracket against the wheel, 0.90 mm nominal across 2 interfaces, so
0.30 mm at worst case - and it holds that value right through the manoeuvre,
because those two turn together. Control-tested by loosening the service to
+/-0.5 mm, which closes 14 pairs.

That closes item 5. With item 6 closed earlier today, **every item on the road
to order that does not need hardware or a decision is now done.**

## 19. A 5 mm error that survived by being unused

The bought-box check covers servos. It does not cover the wheel, so the same
question got asked by hand: which numbers describe the wheel, and how many
copies are there?

```
                sim          cad/wheel.py   cad/envelope.py
WHEEL_R         0.040        40.0           40.0             agreed
WHEEL_HALF_W    0.012        12.0           12.0             agreed
tyre wall       0.008         3.0           -                DISAGREED
```

**The sim said the tread is 8 mm thick. The CAD builds 3 mm.**

That number is not decorative. The whole wheel design turns on it: the rigid
rim stops at `R - TYRE_T` = 37, and `cad/printability.py` asserts no rigid
material outside r = 38.5, because in foot mode the robot has to stand on
rubber rather than on plastic - a fault this part has already been redesigned
around once. At 8 mm the rim would stop at 32 and that assertion would be about
a different wheel.

**It survived because nothing read it.** One definition, zero uses, wrong by
5 mm, sitting in the file that describes this robot to the physics.

> **A constant with no uses is not harmless.** It is a wrong answer waiting for
> the first person who trusts it, and it reads as verified because it sits
> among numbers that are. This is the mirror image of the check that cannot
> fail: a fact nobody consults, which is therefore never contradicted.

And the two that agreed carried comments reading "the sim's WHEEL_R" - an
author seeing the duplication, writing it down, and copying the number anyway.

`cad/wheel_dims.py` now holds all three, mirroring `cad/servo_dims.py` exactly
and for exactly the same reason: it imports nothing, so `src/rsbot/model.py`
can use it without dragging build123d into every physics load. All four sites
import it and all four now agree by construction rather than by luck.

## 20. Every mesh in the project was broken, and the FEA was too low

This is the finding of the day and it retracts two earlier sections.

Correcting `SEG_INERTIA` changed the design loads, and the leg assembly's solve
stopped converging. Chasing that turned up two things in order:

**gmsh's parallel mesher is nondeterministic.** The same STEP with identical
settings gave 49217, 49278 and 49370 nodes on three consecutive runs. At one
thread it gives 49274 every time. Nothing could be diagnosed while "does this
converge" was a coin flip, so `cad/fea.py` meshes single-threaded now. The
solve is still capped; that is where the time goes.

**And then, with a reproducible mesh: every part had INVERTED ELEMENTS.**

```
thigh -0.163   shin -0.495   ankle_yoke -0.562   roll_bracket -0.443
chassis -0.261   leg_assembly -0.622            wheel_body +0.016
```

Negative element quality means a tet with a flipped Jacobian. `Mesh.Optimize`
fixes straight tets; second-order tets then get their mid-edge nodes projected
onto the CAD surface, and on a tight feature that projection pushes a node past
the far face and turns the element inside out. `check_ordering`'s own docstring
notes edges across small bores bowing by nearly half their length. This is what
happens when one bows further.

An inverted element contributes negative stiffness, so the matrix is not
positive definite. **The solves mostly converged anyway.** They returned
numbers that looked like results, and those numbers are in every stress
document this project has ever written.

Fixed with `Mesh.HighOrderOptimize = 1`, chosen by measurement: on the shin, 1
and 2 clear it, 3 leaves 7 inverted, 4 leaves all 23, and netgen makes it worse.
All seven parts now mesh with zero inverted elements at the same node counts.
`mesh_step` refuses to return an invalid mesh at all.

The fix also had to survive itself: gmsh's high-order optimiser `std::terminate`s
when it cannot hit its target, which is not catchable and takes buffered output
with it - a convergence study died leaving two lines and no clue which part.
`General.AbortOnError = 0` turns that into a mesh the guard can reject in words.

### What it did to the answers

| part, PA6-CF | on broken meshes | valid | converged |
|---|---|---|---|
| roll bracket | 70% | **99%** | 57.00 / 56.41 / 56.40 MPa |
| shin | 60% | **83%** | 47.59 / 47.83 / 48.07 MPa |
| leg assembly | never converged | 45% | - |

Both flat to 1% across three densities, so these are answers.

**Section 15 of this report is retracted.** It said both thin margins had turned
out not to exist. They exist, one of them is thinner than the 94% that caused
the worry, and the sentence "every part in this robot is comfortable in the
material it will actually be printed in" was false when written.

Fatigue is fine - the roll bracket is at 74% of its endurance limit - so this is
a static ultimate problem.

> The mesh was the input to every strength claim in this repo, and nothing had
> ever looked at it. **A check validates its logic and forgets its input**;
> that is the same failure as `fasteners.py` checking that a hole was parallel
> to a shaft rather than that it arrived at one, one layer further down.

`docs/road-to-order.md` has a new blocking item 8: the roll bracket needs
geometry, and no printed part should be ordered until it is decided.

## 21. And nothing had ever asserted the static margin

The suite went green again with the corrected inertia and valid meshes. Which
raised the obvious question: **if a part is at 99% of allowable, why is the
suite green?**

Because nothing tested it. `tests/test_stress.py` had a mesh-convergence test
and a fatigue test - both downstream of a static utilisation that no test ever
read. `cad/stress.py` printed the table and `docs/stress.md` quoted it, so the
number looked thoroughly checked. It was thoroughly PUBLISHED, which is not the
same thing.

`stress.statics()` now returns the same shape as `stress.fatigue()`, and
`test_no_part_exceeds_its_allowable_in_the_material_we_would_order` asserts it.

The bound is 100%, deliberately the weakest anyone could argue for, because
what margin this project should REQUIRE is a decision and not a fact. The roll
bracket passes at 99% and is not acceptable at 99%. Tighten it when item 8 is
settled.

**It costs nine minutes**, because it solves every part in every material, and
the suite goes from about 10 minutes to about 19. That is the price of the most
important number in the project having a test at all, and it is worth it.

> Three of today's findings are the same one: `fasteners.py` checked a hole's
> direction but not its destination, `assemble_check` had a sweep nobody ran,
> and the strength table had no test under it. **The gap is never in the check
> you are looking at. It is in the space between checks**, and that space is
> invisible because every individual check passes.

## 22. Looking at it found things no check did

A viewer went in (`cad/fitview.py`, localhost:8783) and the first three faults
in it were found by a human looking at the screen, not by anything measuring.
Worth recording, because this project's instinct is to write a check.

**The servos were boxes.** The viewer took them from `assemble_check`, which
draws oriented bounding boxes on purpose - that is the conservative shape for
interference and the wrong shape to look at. Now `servo.drawing_solid()`,
oriented by the same `SERVO_MESH` table the sim uses.

**The belt was a clearance envelope.** `cad.belt.swept()` is a box the width of
the big pulley spanning the whole centre distance, plus two discs. It is right
for "what must nothing else occupy" and it renders as a featureless grey slab
floating a millimetre off everything, which is exactly how it was reported. Now
two real pulleys and a real belt band, 5.1 cm3 against the envelope's 32.5.

**And the right leg's roll shaft was in front of the tyre.** Placed from the
joint axis, and MuJoCo reports `ankle_roll_r`'s axis NEGATED: the left shaft
sat at x = -57.5..-40 and the right at +40..+57.5. The robot mirrors in Y. The
wheel spans x = -40..40, so it was a 17 mm rod hanging in front of the wheel.
It is now placed from the yoke's own frame, where `cad/ankle.py` already states
where it lives, which cannot pick up a sign that belongs to the physics.

> **A check that measures the right number can still be looking at the wrong
> robot.** Nothing was wrong with the geometry the checks were run on; the
> viewer was assembling a different one, and the only thing that noticed was an
> eye. Screenshot the thing you built.

### And what the viewer needed was not drawn at all

The reason it was worth building: the shin and the ankle yoke had **nothing
between them**, because the joint - a 623ZZ and a 3 mm shaft - existed only as
holes. `shin.py` cuts a counterbore, `ankle.py` cuts a bore, and no solid was
ever placed in either. The same was true of every non-servo bought part:
4 bearings, 4 shafts, 4 pulleys, 2 belts, 10 metal horns. Ten line items, about
$80, none of them modelled.

`cad/hardware.py` draws them, and it produced a number the order sheet never
had. Shaft lengths, from the seats rather than chosen:

```
ankle pitch   bore 14.00 mm   cut 16.0
ankle roll    bore 15.50 mm   cut 17.5
```

The sheet says "30 mm, or cut from stock". It is stock, and one rod makes two.

Two wrong ways to measure that, both recorded in the module: probing MATERIAL
along the axis gave a 120 mm roll shaft, because it ran the length of the wheel
and the roll servo - a shaft passes through holes, not through material. Then
scanning for coaxial bore faces missed the shin's pitch bore entirely and gave
10 mm for a 14 mm joint. The bore is real: 350 mm3 of material rings it and its
wall is exactly `SHAFT_R` from the axis. The scan was wrong, not the part.

**Still not drawn: the ten servo horns.** Every horn joint in this robot -
knee, ankle roll, wheel - is a part bolted to a metal disc, and the disc is not
in the model. It is the same hole this section is about, one layer along.
