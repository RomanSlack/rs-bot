## What changed today

**No CAD, no controller, no tests.** Documents only, and the same 72 tests as
yesterday, still green.

But after eleven weeks of simulation, **hardware is on the way.** Two servos
were ordered this afternoon and arrive 2026-07-31.

Today went on three "why not just..." questions that had been sitting unasked,
all now closed with numbers, and then on actually placing the order those
answers pointed at. The unrecorded decision is the one that gets re-litigated
by whoever picks this up next, and on this project that is usually me.

## 1. Why the servo case pattern can never be measured

Yesterday recovered the horn pattern by measuring three parts that *bolt to*
the horn, six patterns agreeing to 0.00 mm. The obvious follow-up is why the
same trick fails on the case. The answer generalises well past this servo:

> **A dimension is recoverable only if somebody was forced to mate with it.**
> Agreement between independent parts is the measuring instrument. Where no
> agreement exists, no amount of effort produces the number.

Nothing in the reference design bolts to the STS3215 case. TheRobotStudio's
holder is a cradle that the servo drops into. So the case holes were never a
mating dimension for anyone, nobody had to get them right, and there is nothing
to cross-check against. `cad/servo.py` already records the fact; what is new is
knowing it is **structural rather than a gap in the search**. Stop looking. It
costs $15 and a set of calipers.

The corollary is the uncomfortable one. Feetech never publishing a dimensioned
drawing has never hurt anybody, because the people using this servo in volume
do not use those holes. rs-bot puts 40 M2 screws into them. That, not the
measurement, is the open question.

## 2. Building our own actuators: rejected, on mass

The question was whether to build QDD actuators (frameless BLDC, low-ratio
planetary, encoder, FOC driver) like the real robotics companies do, which
would incidentally delete the problem above by making every dimension ours.

Mass is the binding constraint and nothing else gets a turn:

| | |
|---|---|
| STS3215 | 55 g, x10 = 550 g of 1720 g, already 32% |
| smallest credible DIY equivalent | 130-180 g each, so 1.3-1.8 kg |
| delta | about +1 kg on a 1.7 kg robot |

That is not a swap, it is a different machine, and every one of the 64 commits
of FEA, mass budget, CoM trim, balancer gains and leg IK is sized against the
current inertia. Supporting reasons, none of which are needed:

- **Manufacturing basis.** This build is FDM PA6-CF at the service's quoted
  +/-0.3 mm. A planetary needs centre distances and bearing seats at roughly
  +/-0.02 mm. That is machined aluminium, and the whole `cad/` verification
  layer stops applying.
- **Cost.** Ten servos is about $150. Ten DIY actuators is $1500-2500.
- **Control.** Serial bus writes become FOC at 10-40 kHz, current sensing, CAN,
  thermal limits, per-motor calibration. The balancer is built around the
  velocity-command constraint on purpose (README design commitments).
- **The motivating use case is already ruled out.** QDD exists for hopping and
  impact. No hopping is a design commitment, and backlash, the other good
  argument, is already solved in sim at 2-3 deg with `kp` 10.4 and an 80 ms
  low-pass (`docs/backlash.md`).

**The finding worth keeping**, because it is not obvious and it inverts the
usual framing:

> At 1.8 kg the integrated hobby servo is **mass-optimal, not a compromise**.
> 55 g buys motor, gearbox, driver and encoder in one case, and nobody makes
> that in a pancake form factor at this size. Circular actuators start winning
> somewhere around 5-10 kg, where frameless torque density beats integration.
> This robot is below that line.

Buying QDD-class instead of building it fails identically: the smallest
off-the-shelf units are 200-300 g each.

## 3. One central multi-axis driver: rejected, on wiring

Next question: why not one custom board driving as many motors as we like,
instead of a driver per motor.

**What cannot be shared.** Each BLDC needs its own 3-phase inverter: three
half-bridges, gate drivers, current sensing. FOC modulates three phase voltages
at *that* rotor's electrical angle, so two motors at different angles need
different voltages at the same instant. There is nothing to time-share. A
central board relocates the power stage, it never removes it.

**What can.** MCU, power input, CAN transceiver, PCB. This is real: ODrive v3
drove two motors from one board. The ceiling is lower than it looks, though.
Each inverter needs an advanced timer with three complementary PWM channels and
hardware deadtime plus synchronised ADC sampling, and an STM32G4 has two or
three. So **2-3 axes per MCU** at a full-rate current loop, not ten.

**What kills it here is wiring.** Centralising means three phase wires plus
encoder lines from torso to every joint: 70-90 conductors crossing joints,
including the ankle roll that turns 90 degrees. That is on the order of
**100 g of copper landing distally**, which is the worst place for it. Then SPI
encoder lines run 300 mm alongside phases switching amps at 20 kHz, which fails
intermittently rather than cleanly. Compare `cad/wiring.py`: this project is
already fighting to route a **4-wire** chain and still has one unsolved run.

**And the punchline.** We already have the architecture being asked for. The
STS3215 *is* one board commanding ten motors over four daisy-chained wires. It
works precisely **because** the driver is distributed into each servo. You get
the single control board by giving up the central power stage. Corroboration:
ODrive went from dual-axis on v3 to single-axis on S1 and Pro as they moved
upmarket.

Centralising is right when motors sit within ~150 mm of the board: arm at the
base, delta, gimbal, bench rig. Not a leg.

## 4. And then the horn turned up in a product listing

The best thing today arrived by accident. A reseller's page for this servo
publishes a **dimensioned drawing of the horn**, the document this project
assumed did not exist publicly and spent weeks working around.

It confirms yesterday's derivation independently:

| drawing | `cad/servo.py` | agreement |
|---|---|---|
| 4-M3, Ø3.2 | `HORN_SCREW = "M3"`, `HORN_SCREW_R = 1.60` | exact |
| Ø14 bolt circle | `±4.95 x ±4.95`, radius √(4.95²+4.95²) = 7.000 | 0.003 mm |
| 25T (5,9) | `SHAFT_R = 2.95` | exact |
| Ø6.05 countershaft | `IDLER_R = 3.00` | 0.05 mm |

The M2.5 to M3 correction that drove yesterday's entire fastener rework is now
backed by the manufacturer rather than inferred. Inference and drawing agree,
which is the best outcome available: the method was sound *and* the answer was
right.

**It also carries two dimensions the project never had, and both are live in
`cad/wheel.py`.**

1. **The horn pocket has 0.025 mm of clearance per side.** `wheel.py:50` sets
   `HORN_R = 10.0`, cutting a Ø20.00 pocket. The horn is **Ø19.95**. From a
   service quoting +/-0.3 mm, roughly half of prints would not accept the horn
   at all. Every other clearance in the robot was opened up for exactly this
   reason yesterday (0.1 to 0.9 mm); this one survived only because the horn OD
   was unknown.
2. **The pocket may be shallower than the horn is thick.** Line 153 cuts 4.0 mm
   deep. The drawing's section shows 4.5 / 3.6 / 2.5. If the seating flange is
   4.5 the horn stands 0.5 mm proud and the wheel cannot seat flat, which is
   precisely the fault caught yesterday on the wheel-drive servo, where a 0.5 mm
   standoff put the reaction couple into the bolts in bending. If 2.5 is the
   flange, it is fine.

**Neither is fixed**, because both come off a low-resolution image on a
reseller's page rather than a Feetech datasheet. They are leads, and calipers
settle them on Thursday.

Two smaller things fell out:

- **`cad/assembly.py` is stale.** It carries `SERVO_L, SERVO_W, SERVO_H =
  45.2, 24.7, 35.4`, the superseded guesses from `servo.py`'s `OLD` dict, plus
  `HORN_T = 3.0`. Nothing imports it, so it is an orphaned visualisation script
  rather than a design fault, but anyone who runs it gets pre-correction
  geometry.
- **The countershaft end takes its own metal plate**, on the same 4-M3 Ø14
  pattern. The project models the idler boss and never bolts to it. A link
  could be carried in double shear off two metal plates instead of cantilevered
  off one horn. Free, and nobody has considered it.

## 5. The order, placed

**2 x DIYmall STS3215 12 V 30 kg, "+ Metal Bracket" SKU, $65.98, arriving
2026-07-31.** The first money this project has ever spent.

Three things settled it.

**The part number is C018.** Feetech sells at least five STS3215 variants that
are visually identical and not interchangeable: C018 is 12 V / 30 kg.cm /
1:345, and the rest are 7.4 V at other ratios. Most listings that surface first
are the 7.4 V ones, because that is what SO-ARM100/101 uses. `order-sheet.md`
previously said "Feetech STS3215 (12 V, 1:345)", which is not enough to order
from safely. It now carries the part number and the variant table.

**The stock horn may be POM.** Listing spec text describes the bundled horn as
plastic. If true it is useless here, because the whole basis for dropping
inserts from 48 to 16 is that the M3 thread lives in metal. The "+ Metal
Bracket" SKU ships both metal rudder plates and costs $3 over a plain single,
which made the choice trivial. Unconfirmed, and worth checking in the box.

**Two, not one.** One servo *measures* the case pattern; two *check* it. Same
instrument that recovered the horn. With 40 M2 screws planned into those holes,
a disagreement between two units would itself be the finding.

**And a lead to check before anything else is unpacked.** The kit includes
Feetech's own metal brackets. Section 1 above argues the case pattern is
unrecoverable *because nothing bolts to it*. If any bracket in that box bolts
to the **case** rather than to the horn, then it is a mating part, it encodes
the pattern by construction, and section 1 is wrong in the most useful possible
way. Open the box and answer that first.

## The bench actuator, separately

**Build one actuator on the bench, for the experience, off the robot.** About
$85: an iPower GM3506 (encoder already mounted concentric, which deletes the
worst beginner step), an ST B-G431B-ESC1, and SimpleFOC. An evening to spin it
closed-loop, a weekend to trust its position control.

Justified beyond curiosity: it teaches backdrivability and backlash *in the
hands*, and backlash is the finding that most changed this project's plan.
Printing a 6:1 planetary afterwards uses the build123d toolchain that is
already here.

Prices are from memory and want checking before ordering.

the largest line on the order is currently the one nobody has priced.

about **$60 in parts, $100 including calipers**.

## Loose ends found today, not fixed

- **The mass budget has drifted.** `docs/mass-budget.md` says 1720 g and PETG
  at 60% infill; the 2026-07-27 status says 1875 g and PA6-CF. One is stale and
  nobody has said which. Every mass number above uses the 1720 g figure, so it
  moves if the doc does.
- **`pytest tests -q` takes 9.5 minutes, not the ~8 s the README claims.**
  Measured today: **72 passed in 568.41 s**, all green. That is about 70x the
  documented figure, so the README line dates from before the stress and FEA
  checks landed. It matters because "~8 s" is what makes a suite something you
  run on every edit, and 9.5 minutes is what makes people stop running it. The
  README is not corrected yet, and the real question is whether the slow checks
  want a marker so the fast ones can still be run in seconds.

## Next step: measure, on 2026-07-31

Yesterday said "stop simulating and spend the $30". That is done. The next step
is no longer a decision, it is a **measurement list**, and it should be worked
in this order because each item unblocks something downstream.

1. **Does any bracket bolt to the servo CASE?** If yes, the case pattern is
   recoverable after all and section 1 is happily wrong. Answer before
   measuring anything by hand.
2. **Backlash, before assembling anything.** Command hold, twist the output,
   read the encoder back. 12 bits is 0.088 deg, so it resolves 2-3 deg easily
   and needs no caliper, only the bus adapter. This is the number the whole
   design sits closest to.
3. **The case mounting pattern**, from both servos independently. Pin the
   holes, measure over the pins, subtract a pin diameter. Calipers cannot
   measure positions directly. A flatbed scanner at 1200 dpi is 0.021 mm per
   pixel and captures the whole pattern at once.
4. **Horn OD and flange thickness**, against the Ø20.00 x 4.0 mm pocket in
   `cad/wheel.py`. Two possible faults are waiting on this one number.
5. **Is the bundled horn metal or POM?**
6. **The central horn screw**, which appears nowhere in the CAD and is the only
   screw actually fitted at a horn joint on the assembled robot.

Then update `cad/servo.py`, flip `CASE_MOUNT_VERIFIED` if earned, decide servo
capture, route the roll-to-wheel cable, and only then order the other eight
servos and quote the prints.

## The rule, unchanged

> A check that cannot fail is worse than no check at all, because it reads as
> evidence.

No checks were added today, so there is nothing to break on purpose. But the
shape of the day was the same one: each "why not" was settled by finding the
number that binds, not by argument. Mass killed the custom actuators, conductor
count killed the central driver, and absence of consensus killed the case
pattern. Any of the three would have survived a discussion.

**And the day ended by arguing with itself, which is the part worth keeping.**
Section 1 proved at length that the horn pattern could only ever be recovered
by consensus between mating parts. Section 4 then found the manufacturer's own
drawing on a shopping page, and it agreed to 0.003 mm. The reasoning was sound
and the answer was right, and the drawing was still sitting in public the whole
time. Look for the document before proving it cannot exist.
