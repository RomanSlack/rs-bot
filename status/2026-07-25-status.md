## What it is

A desk-scale wheeled biped, 427 mm tall and 2.05 kg, whose **wheels rotate flat
to become its feet**. It balances on two wheels; when it needs to hold still or
manipulate something, each ankle rolls 90 degrees, the wheels lie down, and
their 80 mm faces become the soles.

Everything below is simulation. No parts have been ordered.

## The three mechanisms, and why the third one won

This was the central problem and it took three tries.

| | flat plate on the axle | outrigger struts | **wheel flip** |
|---|---|---|---|
| dig during deploy | 11-28 mm | 0 mm | **1.76 mm** |
| goes airborne | yes | no | no |
| polygon vs CoM | behind it | +8.5 mm forward | **centred, +/-40 mm** |
| extra parts | sole + servo | strut + pad + servo | **none, just a joint** |
| result | fell, 60/60 configs | fell during shift | **stands 87 s** |

The wheel flip wins because its support polygon is **centred under the axle**,
which is where the CoM already is when the balancer hands over. Both earlier
designs died trying to buy fore/aft margin they could not afford.

Checked against the [AgiBot X2-N paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21541v1)
afterwards: they use an actively driven ankle roll joint too, and their
transition takes about 1 s against our 0.79 s. Two things they do that we do
not: a **retaining clip** that locks the wheel twist, and transforming **one
leg at a time in the air**, which needs a hip roll joint we do not have.

Two decisions that fell out of the sim rather than a catalogue:

- **STS3215s are enough.** Worst-case RMS torque is 0.36 N.m at the knee
  against a 0.6-0.9 N.m continuous rating. Peaks touch the 2.9 N.m stall clamp
  for 0.1-1.8% of the time, which is what stall torque is for. No upgrade
  needed, and the QDD tier is built for 20 kg robots, not 2 kg ones.
- **Run the loop at 100 Hz, and do not read every joint back.** It needs 50 Hz
  minimum and dies at 25. Ten servos on one TTL bus will not give 200 Hz if you
  read all ten each cycle. The balancer only needs the IMU and the two wheel
  velocities: sync-write ten, read two.

## Open items, roughly by how much they matter

1. **Gear backlash is the dominant risk.** Under 1 deg it stops mattering;
   over 2 deg it dominates everything. The STS3215 measures 0.87 deg, which is
   *just* inside, with no margin. Two cheap mitigations before spending on
   better hardware: turn down the firmware deadzone (10 counts = 0.88 deg at
   the output, an EEPROM setting), and copy X2-N's retaining clip, which would
   remove the lash at the ankle in exactly the two poses where foot mode
   depends on rigidity.
2. **The ankle-pitch drive is not coaxial with its joint.** The wheel owns that
   axle, so the servo sits high on the shin and needs a belt down to the ankle.
   Not drawn, not sized. This is the one genuine hole in the layout.
3. **Mass distribution is stale.** The shin carries 100 g on the old assumption
   that the wheel servo lived there; it is really on the roll bracket, 110 mm
   further out. Moving 55 g that far out raises leg inertia and needs the
   balancer re-checked. Worth doing before committing to a print.
4. **Turning then immediately flipping fails at 0.8-1.0 rad/s.** 0.6 and below
   is reliable, and oddly so is 1.2. Not chased down.
5. **Never tested:** terrain of any kind (the floor is a perfect friction
   plane), bus latency and jitter, speed-dependent servo torque, roll
   disturbances.

## What sim has been right to be paranoid about

Five bugs this session presented as control failures and were really geometry:

- the shin collided with its own wheel, 26 mm deep, because inserting the ankle
  body made them grandparent/grandchild and MuJoCo only auto-excludes direct
  parent-child pairs
- the robot stood on its ankle brackets, which hung 16 mm below an axle whose
  foot face is only 12 mm down
- the shin capsule's end cap reached under that same face
- lash joints interleaved into the joint ordering, so a hand-written keyframe
  scrambled onto the wrong joints
- the CoM trim assumed body index 1 was the torso, which stopped being true the
  moment a scale-reference human was added

And two that were my measurement, not the robot:

- a tuning cost that penalised falling at 100+ and oscillation at 3x, so the
  search returned gains that never fell and limit-cycled at +/-16 deg
- a connectivity tolerance of 4 mm, right for a running clearance across a
  joint and far too generous inside a rigid part, which hid five bodies that
  were not one piece

The recurring lesson: when the robot falls over, check what is touching the
floor and whether the pose is what you think, before touching a gain.

## Tooling

```
uv run python -m pytest tests -q   # 43 checks, ~30 s
uv run python serve.py             # live sim, localhost:8781, drag to orbit
uv run python render.py            # demo video to renders/
uv run python scale.py             # true-scale still beside a person
uv run python fitcheck.py          # part fit: overlap, assembly, flip sweep
uv run python tune.py [lash_deg]   # re-tune the balancer
```

The fit checks are worth knowing about. MuJoCo cannot answer "do these parts
fit", because geoms on the same body never collide and the visual build is
non-colliding by design. `fitcheck.py` does its own oriented-box test and
answers three separate questions: do parts interpenetrate, is each body one
rigid piece, and does anything collide **during** the flip rather than only at
its end poses. That last one caught the wheel-drive servo sweeping 20.7 mm
through the shin.

## Suggested next step

Order the ten servos and start printing, but **measure the backlash on arrival
first** and turn the firmware deadzone down before assembling anything. That
one number decides whether the foot mode works, and it is the only remaining
input the simulation cannot supply.

Before that, the cheap sim job worth doing is item 3 above: fix the mass
distribution and re-verify, so the printed parts are sized against the real
leg inertia rather than a stale one.
