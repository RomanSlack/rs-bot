## What CAD found that simulation could not

Twelve faults. Grouped by what kind of blindness caused them.

**Geometry that was never drawn.** The shin's knee hub sat 14 mm off the knee
axis, so the part would not have pivoted about its own joint - no stress margin
saves that. Its four M2 horn bolts were inside the bore, cutting air, so it had
no attachment at all. A lightening pocket cut a 10 mm slot through a 20 mm
spine and silently invalidated the stiffness figure quoted for it.

**Loads nobody was carrying.** There is no hip roll joint, so the entire
lateral moment at the hip - 9.5 N.m at the design load - is reacted by the
structure alone. That, not any servo torque, is what sizes the thigh, and at
the original width the margin was 2.0x, the thinnest in the robot.

**Reaction torques through fasteners.** The ankle servo's bolts were 12 mm
apart. Its 1.63 N.m reaction is a force couple, so that is 136 N per bolt. At
40 mm it is 41 N. This is the class of fault that survives assembly and fails a
week later.

**A mechanism that geometry forces.** The ankle-pitch bearing has exactly one
valid location: everywhere else on its axis is inside the wheel in one of the
wheel's two shapes. The servo cannot reach there, so the belt is not a
convenience. And the belt is then pinned from both sides - below 2:1 the belt
is overloaded, above 2:1 the servo runs out of travel. **GT2 2:1, 20T to 40T on
15 mm belt** is the only arrangement that satisfies both.

**Checks that could not fail.** Two of them, and both read as evidence:

- The fit checker skipped same-body pairs as lap joints. Correct for two
  printed pieces bolted together, wrong when one is a *bought part* - a bracket
  cannot lap-joint into a servo case. It was hiding the ankle yoke sitting
  2597 mm3 inside the roll servo, and fixing it surfaced five more.
- The first whole-robot interference check dropped meshes into a MuJoCo scene
  and counted contacts. It reported a clean zero, and kept reporting a clean
  zero with a part shoved 100 mm into the middle of the robot, because MuJoCo
  skips collision between bodies that are all static children of world.

Both replacements are control-tested now. That is the habit worth keeping:
before trusting a check, make it fail on purpose.

## The mass correction, and what it cost

Hand-assigned masses had the robot at 2050 g with 400 g of "printed
structure". The real figure is **165 g** - estimating structure by eye
overshoots badly. The old numbers also left 100 g in the shin for a wheel servo
that actually lives on the roll bracket, 110 mm further out, because a servo
case bolts to the body *proximal* to the joint it drives.

Losing 294 g was not free:

- the balancer needed a **multi-start re-tune**; the old gains fell over at
  2 deg of backlash once the robot got lighter
- **shove rejection is 1.0 N.s, not 1.4** - the same impulse is a bigger
  velocity change on a lighter robot
- the round trip now survives **1.75 deg** of lash rather than 2.0

And the re-tune exposed a real bug: `SETTLE` checked an *instantaneous*
velocity derived from wheel odometry low-passed at 122 ms. The check passed
while the robot was still rolling, and the motion appeared mid-flip with the
outer loop already off. The tell was a 20-cycle run that survived at flip rates
1.2 and 5.0 but died at 0.8, 2.0 and 3.0 - coincidence, not margin. The quiet
condition must now hold for 350 ms, after which all five rates complete 20/20.

## Tooling

```bash
uv run python -m pytest tests -q      # 45 checks, ~40 s
uv run python serve.py                # live sim, localhost:8781
uv run python -m cad.robot wheel      # whole robot from the real parts
uv run python -m cad.assemble_check   # every pair intersected, both modes
uv run python -m cad.masses           # mass per body, from geometry
uv run python cad/view.py <part.stl>  # six views of any part
uv run python fitcheck.py             # overlap, assembly, flip sweep
```

`cad/robot.py` places every part by the **simulator's** kinematics, so a
CAD/sim disagreement shows up as a visibly broken picture rather than a quietly
wrong one.

The method is written down in [porting-a-link.md](../docs/porting-a-link.md) -
the ten-step loop, the design rules, and the traps. Every rule in it exists
because something broke.

## Open items, by how much they matter

1. **Backlash remains the dominant risk.** The design tolerates 1.75 deg and
   the STS3215 measures 0.87. That is inside target with no margin. Two cheap
   mitigations before buying anything better: turn down the firmware deadzone
   (10 counts = 0.88 deg at the output, an EEPROM setting), and copy X2-N's
   retaining clip to remove ankle lash in the two poses foot mode depends on.
2. **The belt is specified but not drawn.** Ratio, tooth counts and width are
   settled; the pulleys and idler are not modelled.
3. **Bearing bores are nominal.** A printed bore needs measuring and offsetting,
   usually 0.1-0.2 mm.
4. **Fasteners unspecified.** Heat-set inserts versus self-tapping. Use inserts
   for anything taken apart more than twice.
5. **The chassis is torsionally soft** - open front and back. A rear brace
   would cost a few grams and is not in the sim either.
6. **Beam theory, not FEA.** Unreliable at fillets and hubs, which is where a
   printed part actually cracks.
7. **Never tested:** terrain of any kind, bus latency and jitter,
   speed-dependent servo torque, roll disturbances.

## Suggested next step

Order the ten servos and start printing.

The simulation has now given up everything it can. The remaining unknowns -
real backlash, real bore tolerance, real bus timing, real floors - are all
things only hardware answers, and the first of them decides whether foot mode
works at all.

**Measure the backlash on arrival, before assembling anything**, and turn the
firmware deadzone down. That single number is the one the whole design is
sitting closest to.
